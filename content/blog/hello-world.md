**TL;DR:** I'm launching a place to share what I build and learn — engineering notes, practical guides, and honest write-ups of side projects. The site itself is a fully static Next.js build, costs $0 to run, and is optimized for both Google and AI search engines.

Hi, I'm Oliver. This is my corner of the internet.

For a while my notes lived in scattered places: half-finished drafts in one app, code snippets in another, and anything worth sharing stuffed into 280 characters. This blog is an attempt to fix that — to write things down properly, in public, where they might actually be useful to someone else.

## What to expect

I'm a software engineer who spends most days in TypeScript and the browser, so that's mostly what I'll write about:

- **Build logs** — how side projects actually got made, including the boring parts and the mistakes.
- **Practical guides** — things like shipping a static site that ranks well, or wiring up structured data without losing your mind.
- **Opinions, softly held** — on developer tooling, web performance, and why so many blogs look the same.

### Geospatial and CAD tooling

A large slice of my work sits at the boundary between design files and maps. I have written about converting DWG drawings to KML in the browser, running CAD parsing libraries through WebAssembly, and building small visualisations from public GIS datasets. The common thread is practical: take a format that locks data inside desktop software and turn it into something a browser can display, search, or measure. Where a dataset is public, I publish the cleaned file next to the article so you can reproduce the result instead of taking my word for it. If you have ever fought with a 200 MB drawing just to answer "where are the parcels?", this corner of the site is for you.

### Hong Kong EV charging

Another running series looks at electric-vehicle charging coverage in Hong Kong. That includes a map of public charging stations, a report on where the network is thinnest, and a side-by-side comparison with European coverage. The numbers come from open government and community datasets, and I try to state the snapshot date every single time — charging infrastructure changes quickly, and an undated chart is worse than no chart. If you spot a station that has disappeared or a figure that looks wrong, the fix is usually a small edit to the CSV followed by a rebuild. The [interactive charging map](/interactive/ev-charging-europe/en/) is the clearest example of how the raw data becomes something you can actually explore.

### SEO for small static sites

The third thread is about getting a static site found. Static HTML is fast, but speed alone does not earn traffic. I write about metadata hygiene, canonical URLs, structured data, and the `llms.txt` convention for making content readable to answer engines. These notes are partly a public notebook: I try an idea on this site, watch Search Console for a few weeks, and then write up what actually changed rather than what I hoped would change. The [guide to generative engine optimisation](/blog/optimize-content-for-ai-search-engines-geo/) collects the longer version of that experiment.

## Why this site is the way it is

A few principles shaped the build:

1. **No server, no bill.** Every page is pre-rendered HTML on a free CDN. It loads fast everywhere, and it costs nothing to run.
2. **Findable by humans and machines.** Clean URLs, RSS, sitemap, structured data, and a plain-markdown `llms.txt` so AI assistants can read the site too.
3. **Writing in files, not forms.** Posts are Markdown files in a git repo. That means version history, offline writing, and no lock-in.

If you're curious how the pieces fit together, I wrote about [the full stack behind this site](/blog/how-this-site-is-built) in a separate post.

## Keeping it honest

Data changes, and so do my opinions. Every article carries its original publication date, and where I revise a figure materially I describe the change in the text rather than silently overwriting it. Datasets are linked together with the moment they were downloaded, because a CSV that updates underneath you makes a chart impossible to verify later. When I get something wrong I would rather leave the correction visible than quietly sweep it away — that is also why this site keeps an RSS feed and a steady stream of dated posts instead of treating every article as a finished product.

If you are arriving from a search result and only have thirty seconds, the TL;DR at the top of each post is written for you. If you have more time, the sections below it are where the actual reasoning lives, including the parts that did not work.
